Multi-column co-sorting on Sapphire Rapids

Seven questions, one machine, 4,260 verified measurements. What SIMD buys, where the two sorters cross over, and why the accelerator does not pay — each stated with what it does not cover.

§

What it adds up to

The question was how a multi-column sort should exploit SIMD, SMT and Intel's data-movement accelerators. All three have answers now, and no two are the same kind of answer — which is itself the result.

SIMD buys throughput. SMT buys throughput for one sorter and costs it for the other. The accelerator buys neither — it buys back capacity, and mostly because the last threads were nearly worthless anyway.

SIMD earns its complexity. Against the same sorter sources compiled at one lane, sixteen lanes is 2.86x, monotonic past two lanes — where vectorising is a measured loss. That the loss exists is what makes the rest of the curve credible, and it says a 128-bit-only machine should not vectorise this kernel at all.

SMT is a per-algorithm decision, not a machine setting. Two threads per physical core is worth 1.12x to the samplesort and 0.88x to the index quicksort. A paper reporting one number here would be wrong for half its own results.

The accelerator's own contribution is a rounding error — between −2.5% and +3.6% across twelve measured rungs on three shapes. The useful finding sits next to it: because a saturated node's last threads are worth almost nothing, the sort can give two of twelve back for free and three for 8–22%. That is worth having on a shared machine and it is not a speed claim.

The same framing explains every negative result on this page. An offload competes with whatever else could use the resource it frees. Against an idle core it wins nothing; against a nearly-worthless eleventh thread it wins; and against the fourth thread of a sorter that peaks at four threads it wins spuriously — which is the one claim here that had to be withdrawn rather than published.

Three claims in earlier drafts of this page were wrong and are corrected above: that the accelerator freed a quarter of the machine on its own merits, that smaller DSA chunks would improve pipelining, and that a cores_freed figure of four was real. Each fell to a measurement rather than to an argument, and the measurements that killed them are the ones worth citing.

How does it compare to implementations other people wrote?

confirmed

A sorting paper that only compares against itself has established nothing. The baselines have to be real libraries, on the same data, measured the same way.

On the operation this work is about the claim holds: 40 of 42 multi-column cells go to us, median 2.24x, up to 8.1x. The single-column cells mostly do not, and they are the honest limit.

The structural claim is that a comparator cannot exploit equal runs. IPS⁴o and std::sort see the columns only through one, so they re-compare a prefix already known equal. That is a mechanism, not a tuning artifact — and it predicts exactly where we lose.

Every loss is to IPS⁴o, and they cluster on ten-column TPC-DS keys and single-column u64: the regimes where either the key is wide enough that the gather dominates, or there is no run structure to find at all.

Holds only under
  • avx512_argsort appears only in single-column cells — an argsort cannot express a lexicographic key over several columns. It also writes 8-byte indices where ours are 4-byte.
  • arrow::SortIndices is serial only: Arrow parallelises across plan nodes, not inside this kernel, so it never appears in a six-worker cell.
  • std::sort(execution::par) and ips4o::parallel::sort choose their own thread count; the row records what we asked ours for.
  • Ours is the faster of the two sorters per cell, both configured from Q0. That is a legitimate product only because Q0 emits both configurations from one file.
Q2

Quicksort or samplesort — which, where, and why?

conditional

Two sorters were built. Shipping both is only defensible if there is a rule for choosing between them.

Neither. The quicksort wins 159 of 177 cells serially; the samplesort takes 62 of 177 at six workers. But the crossover is not a shape family — it is a threshold in how much work the tie structure creates.

The column count is too coarse — at eight columns the parallel split is 39 of 62 — and the shape family is not the axis either. low_cardinality_d4 goes to the quicksort while independent_uniform_c16 goes to the samplesort, so cardinality does not order them.

At ≥8 columns in parallel: above 30 ns/row use the samplesort, below it the quicksort. 65 of 65 cells.

What orders them is how much work the tie structure creates, and the serial quicksort's own cost per row measures exactly that. Cheap means the order resolves early and its faster partition wins; expensive means deep tie chains, where the samplesort's bucket classification amortises and its scheduler is the one that scales. The empty band is what makes this a threshold rather than a tendency.

Holds only under
  • The threshold is fitted to this host and is a property of its memory system as much as of the algorithms. A machine with different bandwidth will move it; the fit is recomputed per results directory rather than hardcoded.
  • All 26 cells the rule gets wrong are at two columns, which is why the rule carries a column floor rather than applying everywhere.
  • Phase columns are zero for every row here: this build compiles the phase timers out, which is what a published number requires. The why behind a crossover needs a separate instrumented build.
  • Six workers is one thread per physical core of one NUMA node, pinned. Numbers at twenty-four workers on this host oversubscribed it twice over and are not in this directory.
Q3

What does cluster detection cost, and does offloading it pay?

capacity, not speed

The equal-run scan is the work a data-movement engine should absorb. This is the question the hardware was chosen for.

The offload never makes the sort faster — its own contribution is −2.5% to +3.6% across every shape and rung measured. What it does is let the sort run on fewer threads for less than proportional loss, so a share of the machine comes back. That is a capacity result, and the credit belongs mostly to the shape of the scaling curve rather than to the device.

What is established

The weak claim holds everywhere. At every rung on every shape, the fraction of the machine returned exceeds the throughput lost: 17% of threads for −1.8% to +8.5%, 25% for 8.2–22.5%, 33% for 15.6–25.1%. If your problem is that a sort monopolises a node, this is a real lever.

The device's credit is consistent and small. Never better than −2.5%, occasionally slightly negative. It is a genuine effect and it is not a performance result.

The offload does not make the sort faster. It makes the last few threads redundant — and on this machine they were nearly redundant already.

The trade is a property of the curve, not the accelerator

Read the orange bars: dropping from 12 threads to 10 costs +0.1% on the heavy-hitter shape, +3.6% on the uniform one and +8.5% on Zipf. Dropping to 9 costs +22.3%, +8.7% and +12.3%. The variation across shapes is an order of magnitude larger than anything the device contributes, so which rung is cheap is decided by where that shape's scalar curve flattens.

The heavy-hitter shape shows this most sharply: threads 11 and 12 are worth nothing at all (−0.3% and +0.1% to give up), and then thread 10 is worth 22%. A cliff, not a slope — so an operator wanting to return capacity must measure their own shape rather than adopt a rule of thumb.

Only the samplesort. The quicksort numbers are an artifact

The index quicksort appears to do far better on this ladder — freeing 58% of the machine while getting faster. Its own scaling curve on six physical cores explains why: 1:128.5  2:103.5  3:83.9  4:80.3  5:85.0  6:89.5 ns/row. It peaks at four threads and degrades after. Against a 12-thread baseline it never should have used, every “freed” thread is one that was hurting it, and the correct action is fewer threads with or without an accelerator. Baselined against its own best point, no device configuration wins.

Synchronous, not asynchronous

The blocking offload is the one that works. Three software defects were found getting there, all mine: an idle worker's poll backoff that compared a global counter and therefore never engaged (105 polls per descriptor, now 9.2); a completion routed past the share-threshold split that decides what is worth handing to another worker; and an accounting mutex taken once per span in the detector's hot path. A fourth — a sequential drain that left spans in the per-worker slots — hung the run outright on heavy-tailed keys, which is how the shape sweep found it. The worst inter-quartile range fell from 32.7% to 1.1% across those fixes.

Holds only under
  • The freeing is capacity, not energy. A synchronous worker busy-polls its completion — 28.55s of CPU against the scalar path's 27.82s at the same thread count — so waiting threads still burn their slots. What is returned is the threads the sort no longer runs on.
  • Three shapes at eight columns, u32, four times the last level, on twelve threads of one NUMA node. A six-cell cardinality sweep agreed on the device contribution (−0.4% to −2.8%) and was lost to a destructive re-run before it could be published; its numbers are quoted here from analysis, not from a surviving file.
  • Chunk size does not help, against the pipelining intuition: the synchronous detection phase falls monotonically as chunks grow, 15.8 ns/row at 8 KiB to 11.6 at 512 KiB. That is per-descriptor overhead, not completion latency.
  • One device, one work queue, and no /dev/iax on this host — the IAA backends are absent rather than slow. A host with several queues per node may not serialise the same way.
  • At cardinality 65536 the device receives one range per pass: everything else falls below the offload threshold. Cheap rungs there measure the scalar curve, not the offload.
Q4

How does it scale in threads — and does SMT help?

conditional

Scaling is where a co-sort either justifies its scheduler or does not. SMT is the cheapest capacity on the machine and nobody had measured whether it helps.

Up to the physical cores of one node it scales, and the two algorithms scale differently: the samplesort reaches a median 2.88x of six, the quicksort 2.36x — and 67 of its configurations end up slower than their own one-worker run.

The quicksort is memory-bound enough that siblings evict each other's lines; the samplesort's bucket classification has enough instruction-level slack to fill the gaps. So whether to enable SMT is a property of the algorithm you chose, which makes it a scheduling decision rather than a machine configuration — and it compounds with Q2: the sorter that scales better with threads is also the one that benefits from siblings.

Holds only under
  • Six workers is the whole thread axis available here: one per physical core of one NUMA node. A claim about more threads needs the second node and a different measurement.
  • Two and four workers were measured only on the four-column synthetic shapes and the measured TPC-DS keys. Everything else has 1 and 6 only, so those curves are two points.
  • The row axis (262k → 16.8M) was swept at four columns; the column axis (1 → 16) at 8.4M rows. Neither is a full grid.
  • Speedup is against each configuration's own one-worker row, so the axis is self-normalising and absolute cost divides out.

Q6

What do SIMD and the abstraction actually buy?

confirmed

The work is built on a portable SIMD abstraction across three register widths and three implementation styles. Both halves of that need justifying.

The width buys most of it, the abstraction is not a tax, and vectorising at all is worth 2.86x at sixteen lanes — with a real loss at two.

The loss at two lanes is the most useful number here. It means a machine with only 128-bit registers over 8-byte keys is a machine where this kernel should not vectorise at all — a portability statement the style sweep alone cannot make, because its narrowest cell is still SIMD.

On the abstraction: at the widest register width the three styles sit within 9% of each other, which is the portability result stated positively. At the narrowest, clang_bool costs 1.39x the best style in the same cell — so the axis is worth sweeping rather than assuming, and the mask representation matters exactly where registers are scarce.

Holds only under
  • Lanes = register bits / (8 x key bytes), so the two widths are not independent axes: u32 reaches sixteen lanes at 512-bit, u64 only eight. Every comparison is inside one (register width, key width) cell.
  • On this host tsl::sse and tsl::avx2 mean 128-bit and 256-bit, not the SSE and AVX2 instruction sets: with AVX-512VL the narrow forms resolve to VL-encoded AVX-512. This compares register widths on one ISA and says nothing about a machine that genuinely lacks AVX-512.
  • The scalar baseline is compiled in the same binary with the full -m flag set, so the compiler was free to auto-vectorise it. Where it succeeded these ratios understate what explicit vectorisation contributes — they are the conservative reading.
  • Serial, post-sort discovery, three columns, one configuration per cell. The question is the cell, not the configuration.
§

Open problems

Four, ordered by how much they would change a claim.

1. The asynchronous poll loop  blocks a result

1,431,929 polls for 13,260 descriptors, 99.8% of them empty, each taking the detector's pool lock. This is why the asynchronous offload regresses the samplesort and why it does not scale from six workers to twelve. Until it is fixed, Q3's asynchronous rows measure the poll loop rather than the device, and the negative result should be stated as does not pay as implemented. The synchronous rows are unaffected and are already at parity.

2. IAA is unmeasured  hardware

The second host has three IAA devices, all of whose work queues belong to the in-kernel iaa_crypto driver — type=kernel, so no /dev/iax node and nothing for userspace to submit through. Handing one over needs --type=user and --driver-name=user; setting only the first is silently reverted. Intel QPL is also not yet built, so the iaa_* backends are not compiled in at all.

3. The shape axis Q3 does not sweep  scope

Every Q3 stage measures independent_uniform_cN, which isolates run length and holds everything else flat. That is the right default and it cannot reach the regime a heavy-tailed key creates, where one value's run is a large fraction of the table. --shapes skewed_zipf_s1,heavy_hitter_f90 now exists for exactly this and has not been run.

4. Q0's second descent  method

The tuner does one descent round from a common default. A second — with the base moved to the winner — would say whether the knobs interact, and it is a rebuild rather than a flag.

Method, and what these numbers are not

Every figure is nanoseconds per row — elapsed time divided by row count, never by rows x columns — and the median of at least nine repetitions, resampled in batches of four while the relative inter-quartile range stays above 5%, to a ceiling of 33. The median rather than the mean because the distribution is skewed by scheduler outliers rather than symmetric around them. Each measurement is verified against a reference image before it is timed; a configuration that sorts wrongly produces a drop with a reason and never a number.

Two variances are in play. Repeating inside one process on warm data, the spread is 1–5%. Re-running the whole binary moves the same numbers by 21% serial and 40% parallel — cold caches, fresh allocations, different scheduler state. Everything here is the intra-process spread, which is right for comparing two algorithms in one run and wrong for an absolute throughput claim.

Interference is recorded per row, not screened once. The start-of-run load average says nothing about minute forty of a six-hour suite, so every row carries the involuntary context switches the kernel charged it. The median row here saw a handful per timed pass — the timer tick, a kworker, an IRQ, unavoidable — and 128 of 4,004 rows exceeded 100 per pass, which is where the cost approaches what this harness can resolve. The run was measured inside an exclusive cgroup v2 partition (cpuset.cpus.partition=root) with numactl pinning one thread per physical core of node 0.
